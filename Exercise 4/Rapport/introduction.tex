\section{Emission spectra}
Different atoms have different energy levels, with different spacings,
and are therefore able to absorb and emit different wavelengths of light.
The atom with the easiest energy levels to describe is the simplest atom, hydrogen,
and the corresponding wavelengths are given by the Rydberg formula (neglecting fine structure):
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\lambda} = \mathcal{R} (\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}), \label{eq:Rydberg}
\end{equation}
where $n_i$ and $n_f$ are the initial and final principal quantum numbers, and $\mathcal{R}$ is the Rydberg constant.
\section{Optical spectrometry}
Optical spectrometers are intruments used to investigate the wavelength composition of light from different sources.\autocite{PaschottaSpectrometers} 
The homebuild spectrometer used for this report uses a glass prism to efficiently utalize the wavelength dependant index of refraction.\autocite{PaschottaRefractiveIndex} 
The OceanOptics Flame \autocite{OcOptFlameManual2016} uses a refractive grating instead of a prism, but the general function is the same.\autocite{PaschottaDiffractionGratings} 
The differrence in refraction leads to a fanning of the colors, so that the intensity of each individual wavelength can be measured.
This concept can be used both for emission and absorbance spectroscopy.
Emission spectroscopy is measuring the light something emits by itself,
while absorbance spectroscopy works by letting light pass through a sample, 
and then measuring what wavelengths originally present are missing when the light comes back out of the sample.
\subsection{N-SF11 Equilateral Prism}
It can be show that the relationship between the angle of incidence $\theta_i$, and the outgoing angle  $\theta_o $ is
\begin{equation}
    \theta_o = \sin^{-1} \left(  n \sin\left( 60^\circ - \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{\sin(\theta_i)}{n} \right) \right) \right). \label{eq:Diffraction}
\end{equation}
The index of refraction $n$, can be found using the Sellmeier equation for the N-SF11 material:
\begin{align}
    n^2 - 1 &= \frac{1.73759695\,\lambda^2}{\lambda^2 - 0.013188707} \nonumber \\
&+ \frac{0.313747346\,\lambda^2}{\lambda^2 - 0.0623068142} \nonumber \\
&+ \frac{1.89878101\,\lambda^2}{\lambda^2 - 155.23629} \label{eq:Sellmeier} 
\end{align}
\section{Wien Displacement Law}
Wiens displacement law says that the wavelength at which the intensity is highest $\lambda_\text{max}$, is related to the temperature $T$ in the following way:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_\text{max} T = b, \tag*{\autocite[p. 1304]{YoungFreedman2020}} \label{eq:WienDisplacement}
\end{equation}
where $b \approx 2.898 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{m $\cdot$ K}$ is a constant.
